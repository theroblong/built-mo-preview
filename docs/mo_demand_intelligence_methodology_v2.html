Forecast error depends entirely on which question you ask

The single most important correction in this update. A forecast does not have one accuracy number — it has several, and they answer different business questions. Quoting one without saying which can mislead in either direction.

The hardest question. Nothing cancels.		 Error at item × retailer × week30.8% – 36.6%63.4% – 69.2%

Every percentage in this document is ERROR, not accuracy. Lower is better. A 0.3% figure means the forecast was within three-tenths of a percent of actual — that is 99.7% accurate, not 0.3% accurate.

Where a comparison figure is quoted from elsewhere, please confirm which convention it uses. A reported "7% forecast accuracy" and "7% forecast error" mean opposite things, and the two are used interchangeably across the industry.

Why the range in each row. Each is measured across several forecasting methods we run and compare. The spread is the difference between the best and the current production method — not uncertainty about the measurement.

Why these are all legitimate. A total-volume figure is the right number for production planning and financial commitment. An item-week figure is the right number for replenishment and assortment decisions. Neither is "the" accuracy.

A correction to our earlier material. A previous version of this paper cited 4.4% for a 13-week hold-out. That figure came from a validation method we have since found to be too generous: the model was being scored on quarters it had partly been trained on.

We rebuilt the evaluation so that every quarter is forecast by a model trained only on data available before that quarter began. The numbers above come from that corrected method. They are less flattering and they are real. We would rather hand you a number you can defend.

Section 1

How the forecast is evaluated

The methodology matters more than the model, and it is the single biggest change since the last version.

Seven quarters, each one a genuine hold-out

For every quarter from the start of 2025 through Q3 2026, the model is retrained using only data that existed before that quarter, then asked to forecast the next thirteen weeks. It never sees the answer. Results are compared against what BUILT actually sold.

Every method is measured against doing nothing

The benchmark is deliberately unforgiving: simply carrying last week's sales forward. In published industry studies roughly half of corporate forecasts fail to beat that benchmark, so any method that cannot clear it is not adding value. The clearest result. Every method we run is roughly twice as accurate as a year-over-year comparison (30–37% against 54.5%). That is the comparison that reflects how most demand planning is done today, and it is where the forecast earns its place.

Stated plainly: on mature, high-volume items the machine-learning model does not currently beat a simple carry-forward. We report that rather than select a flattering slice. The sections below explain why, and where the model does add value that no simple method can.

Section 2 — the key finding

BUILT's growth is distribution, not velocity

This single measurement explains most of what we learned, and it has direct consequences for planning.

Comparing the last fifty-two weeks against the fifty-two before:

Source of growthUnitsShare
Items at retailers that did not exist a year ago11,888,26661%
Existing item-retailer combinations growing7,766,08140%
Combinations that stopped selling−275,409−1%

1,008 new item-retailer combinations appeared against 759 continuing ones. Among the continuing ones, the median grew 1.46× across a full year — under one percent a week — with 64% growing and 36% shrinking.

What this means for planning. For an item already selling at a retailer, week-to-week movement is small relative to normal volatility. There is genuinely little trend to project, which is why a carry-forward performs as well as it does.

And the bigger implication: the dominant driver of BUILT's volume is which items reach which retailers, and when. That is decided by authorizations, launch plans and door counts — information that does not appear in sales history at all. No forecasting method applied to past sales can anticipate a combination that has never sold.

A practical consequence we tested

Applying the portfolio's overall growth rate to each individual item made forecasts worse, producing a systematic 10.3% over-forecast. Most of that growth is not happening inside existing items, so applying it to them double-counts. This is the kind of error that is invisible without decomposition.

Section 3 — new analysis, 3 October

Why last January does not predict this January

A forecast that leans on the same month a year earlier assumes the business was comparable then. For a brand expanding distribution this fast, it was not. We measured exactly how far from comparable, and found the structural reason the forecast runs low.

Same-store sales are falling while the business doubles

Restricting to item-retailer-market combinations selling in both a month and the same month a year earlier — the comparable-store basis retail uses — separates expansion from underlying demand:

MonthComparable cellsComparable-basis YoYTotal YoYVolume from cells new in the year
March 20252553.26×4.39×31.3%
August 20252553.91×6.12×41.4%
December 20253441.08×2.40×58.2%
March 20264320.98×2.05×54.4%
August 20266240.73×1.39×47.9%

Comparable-basis growth fell from 3.3× to 0.73× in eighteen months. The same items, in the same accounts and markets, now sell 27% fewer units than a year ago, while the total business still grows 1.39×. Every unit of net growth is coming from newly distributed combinations.

This is why a year-ago anchor cannot carry the forecast. A method that reads last January and scales it is reading a business with roughly half as many selling combinations, each running at a materially different rate. The relationship between the two Januaries is not stable enough to lean on — and it is still moving.

Units per distribution point converged across the whole portfolio

Dividing volume by distribution should isolate per-store demand. Instead it revealed a second movement running the other way, comparing the same four months a year apart:

Because the comparable cells moved just as far as the portfolio, this is not newer, weaker doors diluting an average — the decline happened inside established combinations, and new ones arrive already at the new level. The practical consequence is that distribution points are not a fixed multiplier on volume. Their productivity is itself a moving quantity, so projecting volume from distribution growth alone overstates it.

The structural reason the forecast runs low

A forecast produced in one month cannot contain an item-retailer combination that does not exist yet. We measured how much of each target month's actual volume came from combinations invisible at forecast time:

Forecast horizonMedian share of actual volume from combinations not yet existingMaximum observed
One month ahead2.8%12.1%
Thirteen weeks ahead14.4%25.7%

This explains the shape of the error, and it reframes the accuracy numbers. At a quarter's distance, roughly one unit in seven comes from combinations no history-based method could have known about. That sets a floor on how low a forecast of existing items can run, and it is the single clearest reason the quarterly view reads lower than the one-month view.

It also means the headline error is partly two offsetting errors. The forecast misses new combinations downward while running somewhat high on existing ones, and the two partly cancel. That nets to a usable number but an unstable one — which matches the pattern of occasional large misses rather than consistent small ones.

We tested the obvious fix, and it is not yet a fix

Roughly 1,000 launches are observable in three years of SPINS — far more information about how new items behave than the two Januaries a seasonal method gets. We used them to estimate how many new combinations arrive each month and how much they sell, then added that to the forecast. Five variants were tested across fifteen rolling origins, with every quantity estimated only from data available at the time.

Thirteen weeks aheadSystematic biasMedian monthly error
Existing combinations only−10.7%14.9%
Plus a new-arrival layer−1.3%13.7%

The layer does what it was built to do: it removes almost all of the systematic under-forecast. But it does not yet reduce month-to-month error, because arrival volume itself changed regime inside the test window — new combinations arrived in rising numbers while each one sold less. A correction sized on the earlier period overshoots the later one. We are reporting this as a confirmed diagnosis with an incomplete remedy rather than as an improvement, and the next step is estimating arrival volume directly instead of inferring it from counts.

One clear positive from the same test. A naive carry-forward measured at thirteen weeks misses by 14.9% in the median month. Mo's current model measured the same way misses by 7.6%. Whatever remains to be improved, the model is roughly halving the error a sensible simple method would produce at that distance.

Where the next SKU should and should not go

Brian's three portfolio questions — when does another SKU stop growing an account, where should SKUs be reduced, and should Dreamwich launch as pure incremental distribution or trade out weaker items — are all answerable from the models already running. The output is a two-bucket account triage:

  • Incremental bucket — accounts with a low BUILT SKU count, strong per-SKU velocity and predicted low cannibalization. Dreamwich goes in as pure net-new distribution.
  • Trade-out bucket — accounts already saturated, where marginal cannibalization exceeds marginal volume. Here Mo names the weakest current item — lowest velocity, highest cannibalization contribution — as the displacement candidate.

This is actionable before Dreamwich ships, because the triage runs on current portfolio health. Once launch data flows, Dreamwich's own measured rate sharpens the placement call and validates the decisions made ahead of it.

One honest limit on coverage. Cannibalization is currently scored for about 48% of product-account-market combinations. The cause is a deliberate quality gate requiring eight weeks of history on both sides of a pair before it is scored, so the gap sits mostly on recent launches and on BUILT's newer convenience and club distribution. It closes as those combinations mature.

Section 5

What we tried, and what we learned

Roughly twenty distinct approaches were tested against the carry-forward benchmark on the corrected methodology. Most did not beat it. The negative results are as useful as the positive ones, because they tell us where the limits actually are.

ApproachResult
Optimized exponential smoothingBest at item level. Beats the benchmark by 1.7 points.
Seasonal decomposition (STL, seasonal ETS, Theta, seasonal ARIMA)Cannot be fitted. These require two complete years per item; the data begins October 2023, so no item yet has two full cycles.
Seasonal adjustments and calendar featuresNo improvement. Seasonality is measurable at portfolio level but not yet per item.
Trend extrapolation, and trend combined with machine learningWorse at item level; modest help at portfolio level only.
Alternative statistical objectives and target transformationsNo reliable improvement once tested across the full portfolio.
Deep-learning forecastersPreviously reported; could not be run in the current environment, and the one completed test did not beat simpler methods.

Three lessons worth recording

  • Validation method matters more than model choice. The difference between our old and new evaluation was far larger than the difference between any two models.
  • A result on one retailer is not a result. Several promising improvements appeared on a single account and disappeared when tested across all 2,128 series. Everything is now confirmed portfolio-wide before it is reported.
  • History has a floor. With under two years of seasonal history and most growth coming from items that did not previously exist, there is a limit to what past sales alone can predict. That limit moves as history accumulates, and it moves faster with forward-looking data.
Section 6

What Mo provides that a point forecast does not

Accuracy on a single number is one measure of value. These are capabilities that a spreadsheet process does not produce at all, regardless of its accuracy.

Complete coverage, including new launches

Every item-retailer combination receives a forecast — 2,128 of them, with none skipped. Previously 110 items produced no forecast at all, including the newest launches, which are exactly where distribution and production decisions are being made. A carry-forward cannot meaningfully forecast an item with three weeks of history; the system now routes those to an appropriate method and labels them as such.

Ranges, not just a number

Every forecast carries a low, expected and high figure, so a planner can see where the forecast is confident and where it is not — and size inventory risk accordingly.

Distribution versus velocity decomposition

For any change in demand, the system separates how much came from more stores carrying the item versus more sales per store. Given that 61% of growth is distribution, this is the difference between a number and an explanation.

Cannibalization and price elasticity

When a new flavour launches, how much is genuinely incremental and how much came from BUILT's own existing items — measured per retailer. Price sensitivity is measured per retailer and used for scenario modeling. Neither is available from a sales history alone.

Data quality that compounds

Building the forecast surfaced issues worth more than the model itself. One retailer was being counted twice in the underlying data through two overlapping market definitions, inflating its demand by roughly 2.3 million units a year. Several hundred item-retailer combinations with no sales for over a year were still being planned for. Both are now corrected and guarded against.

In summary

Where things stand

  • Forecast error depends on the question. Total volume error sits between 0.3% and 4.3% (95.7–99.7% accurate); item-level weekly error sits around 31% (69% accurate). Both are real and they measure different things.
  • Every method runs at roughly half the error of a year-over-year comparison (31–37% against 54.5%).
  • Tracked the way a finance team tracks it — forecast vs actual each month — a typical month lands within 5–8%. Fifteen of twenty-one months tested are inside ±8%.
  • The remaining error is concentrated in January–March and November–December, the two windows where annual seasonality dominates and where under two years of history limits what can be learned. The January gap narrowed from −61% to −29% year over year.
  • On mature items the model does not beat a simple carry-forward. We say so rather than select a flattering view.
  • 61% of growth comes from items and doors that did not exist a year ago, which is the main reason sales history alone has limits here.
  • The evaluation method was rebuilt so that every reported figure comes from a genuine hold-out. Earlier figures were optimistic and have been corrected.
  • The clearest path to improvement is forward-looking distribution and promotional data, not a different algorithm.
