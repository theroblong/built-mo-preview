What the Forecast Is Worth

Three decisions BUILT can now make that were not previously possible — and what they are worth.

Prepared for Bracken · 1 October 2026 · All figures from BUILT’s own measured retail sales through 6 September 2026

The forecast is not a single number for the business. It is a number for every product, at every retailer, for every week. That is what makes it usable for decisions rather than reporting.

This note sets out three things that changed, each measured in BUILT’s actual retail dollars: demand we were counting that did not exist, demand we were planning for on products that stopped selling, and the newest products that had no forward number at all.

1. We were counting one retailer twice

Circle K was being reported to us under two overlapping market definitions, and both were being added into the plan. The same cases were counted twice.

$2.29M

of Circle K demand in the last 52 weeks that was never actually there. Circle K’s real measured sales are $2.27M, not the $4.55M the plan was carrying.

What this changes: any volume commitment, trade spend or production plan sized off Circle K was built on roughly double the real demand. Six other retailers — Albertsons, CVS, Kroger, Sam’s, Target and Walgreens — have the same duplicate market definitions sitting in the data today carrying no volume. They are now excluded by rule, so this cannot reappear quietly if one of them starts reporting.

2. We were planning demand for products that had stopped selling

513 product-retailer combinations have recorded no sales for nine weeks or more — half of them for more than a year. The plan was carrying their last known sales rate forward as if they were still selling.

$631K

of demand per year removed from the plan that no longer exists.

These are not written to zero either, which would be equally wrong. BUILT’s own history shows items do come back: of the gaps of this kind we can measure, a meaningful share resumed selling at close to their previous rate. So each one now carries a number weighted by how likely it is to return, and that likelihood falls the longer it stays quiet. Items still on shelf are treated differently from items that were wound down.

What this changes: inventory and production were being planned against demand that had already gone. This is working capital tied up against sales that were not coming.

3. The newest products had no forward number at all

108 products across 462 product-retailer combinations were producing sales but receiving no forecast. They sold $1.81M in the last 13 weeks — 2.5% of recent sales — with no forward view whatsoever. These are BUILT’s newest launches: exactly the products where distribution, production and trade decisions are being made right now.

The range is deliberately wide on a product with only a few weeks of selling history. A wide honest range is decision-useful; a narrow confident number on a brand-new item is not.

Why a single company-level accuracy number cannot do this

A total-volume forecast can be very accurate and still answer none of the questions above, because over- and under-calls on individual products cancel each other out inside the total.

  • It cannot tell you Built Puff Peanut Butter Cup is tracking to $327K over the next quarter at a single retailer, so that retailer’s supply can be sized for it.
  • It cannot tell you $2.29M of Circle K demand was double-counted, because the total was still roughly right once other items absorbed it.
  • It cannot tell you which 513 product-retailer combinations to stop producing for.

Both numbers are useful and they measure different things. Total accuracy answers “did we plan the right overall volume?” Item-level accuracy answers “which product, at which retailer, in which week?” — and only the second one can be acted on. Mo is built to complement the company view, not replace it.

What we would like to confirm

So we can line our numbers up against BUILT’s own, we would like to know how the current forecast accuracy figure is measured:

  • Is it measured on shipments to retailers, or on consumer sales at the register?
  • At what level — total company, brand, or individual product at individual retailer?
  • Over what period ahead — next month, next quarter?
  • Is it total volume variance, or the average miss on each item added up?

These four choices can move a reported accuracy figure by a very large margin, in either direction. Once we know them we can report ours the same way and the two will be directly comparable.
